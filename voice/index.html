Voice & tone

How the site is written

Everything on adamatteia.com is written in one voice: plain, first person, and direct, with a dry edge that stays on. This page sets out that voice, the principles behind it, and how the tone shifts from one part of the site to the next.

The site speaks to several kinds of reader at once, so the writing leads with the problem and the outcome, keeps titles and jargon low, and lets the substance carry. The restraint is the design, not a shortage of things to say. Use Jump to section in the header to move around.

01

The voice

Voice is constant: it is who is speaking. Tone flexes: it is how that voice meets the moment. Start with the voice. It does not change anywhere on the site. In one line: precision with a dry edge.

Where the voice sits

A few spectrums, with this voice marked on each and the reason it sits there. Not a borrowed framework, but the axes that actually describe how the site reads.

SeriousPlayful

The wit is dry, never jokey.

FormalCasual

First person and contractions, but never matey.

Matter-of-factEnthusiastic

Evidence does the persuading, not adjectives.

ReservedDirect

States an opinion without hedging.

PlainElaborate

One idea per sentence.

02

Principles

Seven habits the writing keeps to. Each is paired with a line already on the live site, so these describe what the copy does, not what it hopes to.

01

First person, plain, direct

Adam speaks as himself, in everyday words. No third-person bio voice, no abstraction for its own sake.

“I don’t try to win that argument. I hand them the pen.”
02

Lead with the problem, not the artefact

The interface is never the point. The thing underneath it, and the people who live with it, are.

“The work isn’t the interface. It’s the problem underneath it, and the people who’ll live with whatever gets built.”
03

Use the turn: not X, but Y

Set up an obvious read, then correct it. It is the workhorse construction.

“The fix was completion confidence, not more features.”
04

Let numbers and sectors persuade

Concrete beats adjective. The proof is specific figures and named environments, stated once and plainly.

“−48% customer service calls.”
05

Name the jargon to dismiss it

Constraints and government-speak appear only to be reframed, never to decorate.

“Procurement, classification, legacy infrastructure, legislation. None of that is in the way of good work. It’s what good work has to fit inside.”
06

State opinions flatly

One confident, insider observation per idea. No hedging, no exclamation.

“The person most resistant to a new system usually understands the old one better than anyone in the room.”
07

Earn a short close

End a passage on a clean, declarative payoff.

“Your hardest stakeholder becomes your strongest advocate.”
03

Tone

One voice, meeting different moments. The voice never changes; the tone moves. Warmer in the opening, drier in the principles, terser over the numbers, plainer where the facts have to be exact.

Hero
Warm, declarative

I help teams simplify complex programs and land lasting change.

Principles
Opinionated, dry

Your hardest stakeholder becomes your strongest advocate.

Outcomes
Terse, evidence-led

−48% customer service calls. The fix was completion confidence, not more features.

Availability
Plain, factual

Based in Canberra, NV1 cleared to 2029 and NV2 eligible.

Testimonials
Others’ words, left intact

Attributed by initial and sector, never edited for tone.

Close
Warm, brief

All the best, Adam.

04

Before / after

The same point, written two ways: the draft most senior profiles reach for, and the edited line that ships. Flip between them. The drafts are honest stand-ins, not quotes of anyone real.

05

Mechanics & diction

The smaller rules: how the sentences are built, which words are house words, and one deliberate call about punctuation.

Sentence mechanics

  • Vary sentence length. A longer setup, then a short punch. Rhythm carries the emphasis.
  • Fragments are allowed for emphasis. Used sparingly.
  • Active voice, present tense. “I resolve those first”, not “those are resolved”.
  • One idea per sentence. If it needs a second spliced clause, split it.

Diction & spelling

  • en-AU spelling: colour, organisation, modernisation, behaviour, defence. The one exception is “program”, not “programme”, matching how the site names a body of work.
  • House vocabulary: complex programs, lasting change, land a change, own it, carry it forward, the work underneath. Reach for these first.
  • Contractions are good (isn’t, won’t, I’ll). They keep it human.
On dashes

The Australian Government Style Manual rules the em dash out of government writing and sets the spaced en dash as the house break. The site goes one step further and avoids dashes for breaks altogether, using commas, colons, and full stops. In 2026 a dash mid-sentence has become a quiet tell of generated text to a lay reader, so the site trades a small typographic nicety for trust. A deliberate choice for this moment, not a claim that the dash is wrong.

06

Banned words

The hype register the site refuses. If a word would survive on any consultant’s profile, it is cut.

  • world-class
  • passionate
  • results-driven
  • cutting-edge
  • leverage
  • synergy
  • seamless
  • robust
  • dynamic
  • thought leader
  • delivering value
  • best-in-class
  • game-changing
  • rockstar
07

The checklist

What every new line is checked against before it goes on the site.

  • First person, present tense, plain words
  • Leads with the problem or outcome, not the deliverable
  • At least one turn, or a clean declarative close
  • Every number stated exactly once
  • en-AU spelling; “program”, not “programme”
  • No hype words, and no corporate neutrality
  • One dry, confident line: the edge is still on
